# Multicultural Color Gaps: South Asia, Andes/Mesoamerica, Maghreb/Middle East, Nordic, Mediterranean, Pacific/Māori, Southeast Asia

Scope note: this fills the gaps listed in `multicultural_color.md` §2 and §3. It does not repeat Japan, China, Korea, Persian haft rang, bògòlan, kente, Aboriginal ochres, Barragán, Jonauskaite 2020 or Reinecke & Gajos 2014. The pass used about 20 tool calls, so coverage is uneven. Anything not fetched is listed under Gaps rather than filled in from memory.

## 1. What dye and pigment chemistry gives each tradition its colors, and are there museum or conservation-science measurements?

### Takeaway
Most of these traditions rest on a small set of well-characterised colorants. Reds come from anthraquinones: cochineal (carminic acid), lac (laccaic acids), madder/chay and morinda. Blues and purples come from indigoids: indigo, Maya blue, Tyrian dibromoindigo. Earths and blacks come from iron oxide or iron-tannin complexes, and glazes from metal ions (Co²⁺, Cu²⁺, Mn, Fe, Sn). HPLC, XRF, PIXE, Raman and reflectance studies exist for almost every one. Measurements are almost always published as chemistry or spectra, not as sRGB values. I found few published CIELAB values for these regions; the Tyrian purple dye studies are the main exception.

### Cited Findings

**Andes (Peru/Bolivia/Chile) — cochineal, plant reds, indigo, shellfish purple**
- The Met's Andean textiles essay lists the materials as cotton and camelid fiber, natural dyes and pigments including cochineal, and Amazonian feathers — [Met Heilbrunn essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/andean-textiles)
- Elena Phipps, Met senior textile conservator 1977–2010, wrote *Cochineal Red: The Art History of a Color* (Met Bulletin 67(3), 2010) and "Woven Brilliance: Approaching Color in Andean Textile Traditions" (Textile Museum Journal, 2020). She calls cochineal "the contribution of the Americas to global art history" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260471817_Cochineal_Red_The_Art_History_of_a_Color); [Bard Graduate Center podcast](https://www.bgc.bard.edu/research/articles/685/s2e2-elena-phipps-textile-color)
- Wouters & Rosario-Chirinos (1992, JAIC 31(2):237–255) is the classic HPLC-DAD study of purple, red and blue dyes in pre-Columbian to Inca Peruvian textiles — cited in [Heritage 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-9408/4/3/91)
- *Heritage* 2021 compared DART-MS and paper-spray MS with HPLC-DAD on Met-held fragments. Carminic acid (cochineal) shows up *later*, in Wari and Lambayeque pieces. Some Wari samples contain only plant-red anthraquinones, and one Wari band (2003.40.5) has both indigo and cochineal. So "Andean red = cochineal" is period-dependent, not timeless — [MDPI Heritage](https://doi.org/10.3390/heritage4030091)
- HPLC-MS/MS found nine previously unknown carminic-acid derivatives in pre-Columbian Peruvian textiles dyed with *Dactylopius coccus*. The authors propose them as a regional fingerprint — [Anal. Bioanal. Chem. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00216-014-8107-y)
- In reflectance spectra, cochineal's absorption maxima (about 520–525 and 555–560 nm) are red-shifted compared with plant reds. This is how non-invasive studies tell them apart (Tarapacá, Atacama) — [Heritage Science](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-021-00538-9)
- Atacama coastal textiles (1000–1450 AD) show carminic acid as the main red, with preliminary evidence of cochineal mixed with shellfish purple. Dyed purple yarns appear no later than about 500 AD (citing Phipps). HPLC was not done — [PLOS ONE 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0325623)

**Mesoamerica — Maya blue (and cochineal, above)**
- Maya blue is an indigo–palygorskite clay hybrid, made by heating the mix above about 120 °C. It has lasted more than 1500 years and resists acid and oxidation — [J. Phys. Chem. B](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jp048587h); [arXiv 1207.3242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.3242)
- Dehydroindigo occurs alongside indigo. The pigment is a "polyfunctional hybrid" with indigo/dehydroindigo isomers both on the clay surface and in its channels. The binding mechanism is still debated — [J. Phys. Chem. B 2007](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jp067369g); [Academia (J. Phys. Chem. C 2009)](https://www.academia.edu/58313919/Evidence_of_Topological_Indigo_Dehydroindigo_Isomers_in_Maya_Blue-Like_Complexes_Prepared_from_Palygorskite_and_Sepiolite); [J. Phys. Chem. C time-resolved](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.jpcc.6b04681)
- The clay red-shifts indigo's absorption, so Maya blue is spectrally not the same as indigo on cloth — [J. Phys. Chem. C](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.jpcc.6b04681)

**South Asia — chay/madder mordant reds, iron-tannin black, indigo, lac, Indian yellow**
- Coromandel Coast chintz process (V&A summary via Winterthur/Wandering Silk): alum and iron mordants are painted with a *kalam*. Chay root (*Oldenlandia umbellata*) with alum gives red. Iron with tannin (myrobalan) gives black. Wax resist is followed by indigo for blue. The cloth is dyed through the mordant, not painted with colour. Winterthur's recreation used madder in place of chay — [Winterthur](https://www.winterthur.org/charting-chintz-making-the-fabric/); [Wandering Silk](https://www.wanderingsilk.org/post/2016/05/10/textiles-chintz)
- The *Interwoven Globe* catalogue (Met 2013) says Indian export designs were printed in iron black, filled with mordanted chay red and resist-dyed indigo. It found no evidence then of overdyeing for green. It includes Phipps, "Global Colors: Dyes and the Dye Trade," pp. 120–35 — [Met exhibition](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2013/interwoven-globe); [ResearchGate chapter](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342247852)
- Chay root was gathered in Jaffna (Sri Lanka) for a failed 17th-century VOC chintz venture, which links Sri Lanka into the same dye economy — [Wandering Silk](https://www.wanderingsilk.org/post/2016/05/10/textiles-chintz)
- Lac dye comes from the lac insect's resin, as a by-product of shellac. Its main colourant is laccaic acid A (C26H19NO12). Lac precipitated with alumina gives "Indian lake". CAMEO lists it as Natural Red 25 / CI 75450 — [MFA CAMEO](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Lac_dye); [Wikipedia: Laccaic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laccaic_acid)
- HPLC-PDA on Met-held Safavid and Mughal velvets found laccaic acids in most red piles. Lac is *not* a valid marker of Indian origin, because Persian carpets used it too. Yellows were inconclusive — [Heritage Science 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-015-0037-2)
- Indian yellow is a separate colorant: euxanthic acid salts (Mg/Ca euxanthate) plus euxanthone — [Wikipedia: Indian yellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_yellow)
- A dye review describes lac's minor yellow anthraquinones (erythrolaccin etc.) as giving its reddish-yellow cast — [Dagur & Ghosh 2025, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09731296241305858)

**Maghreb — zellige glaze colorants**
- PIXE on 31 Moroccan zellige samples (14th–18th c.) found tin (SnO₂) for white, cobalt for blue (Co²⁺), copper for green (Cu²⁺ in a Pb-rich glaze), Fe³⁺/Mn⁴⁺ for brown/black, Mn³⁺ for violet and iron for yellow. Co–As correlation differs by monument (Meknes vs Marrakech), which hints at different cobalt ore sources — [ResearchGate (PIXE)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263904416_PIXE_ANALYSIS_OF_MOROCCAN_ARCHITECTURAL_GLAZED_CERAMICS_OF_14TH-18TH_CENTURIES)
- Raman and optical absorption on 18th-c. Dar El Beïda (Meknes) zellige found Co for blue, Cu for green, cassiterite for white, and hematite + magnetite + MnO₂ for brown — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245334100)
- pXRF at the Saadian Tombs and El Badi (Marrakech) found lead-silica glazes with 25–59 wt% PbO. In the original glazes, phosphorus (not cobalt) gave a blue opalescence; later work switched to Co/Cu — [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/86066507)
- Micro-XRF of Marrakech zellij (13th–20th c.): older tiles are Pb/Sn-rich with Cu greens; modern tiles are Ba-rich with **Cr greens**. So "authentic zellige green" is a copper green, and the modern chromium green differs in both chemistry and hue — [ScienceDirect (Bendaoud et al. 2013)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S096980431300290X)

**Mediterranean — Tyrian (shellfish) purple**
- Shellfish purple is a mixture of indigo, 6-bromoindigo and 6,6′-dibromoindigo, plus indirubins. The bromoindigos are unique to it — [ColourLex](https://colourlex.com/project/tyrian-purple/); [MFA CAMEO](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Tyrian_purple)
- CIELAB plus HPLC study (Structural Chemistry 2017): colour depends on the dye ratio and on *debromination during dyeing*, which varies with bath pH and the light source. Hue is therefore process-dependent and has no single canonical value — [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/103794741)
- In a lightfastness study measured in CIELab, wool held the colour best, followed by silk, linen, nylon and cotton, and deeper shades were most fast — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/239707026)
- The earliest HPLC-confirmed murex purple textile is from Timna, Israel, late 11th–early 10th c. BCE — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7842898/)

**Nordic — Falu red and NCS**
- Falu rödfärg is iron oxide from weathered tailings of the Falun copper mine, which also contain silicates, Cu and Zn compounds. It is washed, dried and burned, and the heat turns yellow iron hydroxide red. It is bound in rye flour, linseed oil and water to make a matte, breathable wood paint. "Falu Rödfärg" is a protected trademark tied to Falun-mine pigment, while generic *rödfärg* can be any ochre or iron-oxide flour paint — [Wikipedia: Falun red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falun_red); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/why-are-all-swedish-cottages-painted-red-180975914/)
- Smithsonian reports that one company holds exclusive rights to the pigment source and that supply may last about a century — [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/why-are-all-swedish-cottages-painted-red-180975914/)
- A competitor claims the paint contains lead. This is unverified and comes from a commercial competitor — [Moose Färg](https://www.moosefarg.com/swedish-red/)
- **The NCS (Natural Colour System)** is itself a Nordic colour tradition. It is a Swedish national standard (SIS colour atlas), a psychometric notation based on Hering's opponent colours with six elementary colours (W, S, Y, R, B, G). It was developed by Hård, Sivik and Tonnquist from 1964 and won the AIC Judd award in 1997. The atlas is sold by NCS Colour AB, so it is proprietary — [Hård & Sivik 1981, Color Res. Appl.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/col.5080060303); [NCS history](https://ncscolour.com/en-int/pages/the-history-of-ncs-colour); [Wikipedia: NCS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Color_System)

**Pacific / Māori — kōkōwai, black, white**
- Kōwhaiwhai (painted rafter patterns) use red, black and white. Red is kōkōwai (red ochre) mixed with shark oil, black was soot, and white was originally bare wood (now paint) — [Te Ara 1966 Encyclopaedia](https://teara.govt.nz/en/1966/maori-art/page-8)
- Te Aka Māori Dictionary gives *kōkōwai* as "red ochre" and as a stative meaning "dark red, crimson, red-brown". Its usage example is about kōwhaiwhai — [Te Aka](https://maoridictionary.co.nz/word/2875)
- A Māori-authored colour-theory page says red, black and white "saturate" Māori visual language across kōwhaiwhai, weaving, whakairo and tā moko — [He Kapunga Oneone](https://www.kauaeraro.com/onamata/ki-te-ao-marama-introduction-to-colour-theory-a-tirohanga-maori)

**Southeast Asia — Javanese batik, Thai pigments**
- Classical Javanese batik uses indigo blue and *soga* browns and yellows from barks and woods. Yogyakarta soga combines jambal (*Peltophorum*), tingi (*Ceriops*, a mangrove) and tegeran (*Cudrania*), with lime (CaO) as the mordant. Soga over indigo gives blue-black. North-coast (*pesisir*) batik used morinda (*mengkudu*) root reds. Old Javanese records name indigo and *wungkudu* (morinda) as the two key dyes (Christie 1993) — [Asian Textile Studies: brown](http://www.asiantextilestudies.com/brown.html); [Asian Textile Studies: morinda](http://www.asiantextilestudies.com/morinda.html); [Batik Guild](https://www.batikguild.org.uk/batik/indonesia)
- Popular sites say soga comes from morinda leaves. The specialist sources disagree: soga is a bark mix and morinda is a separate red — [factsanddetails](https://factsanddetails.com/indonesia/Arts_Culture_Media_Sports/sub6_4b/entry-4050.html) vs [Asian Textile Studies](http://www.asiantextilestudies.com/brown.html)
- Sogan (brown) batik belongs to the inland courts of Surakarta and Yogyakarta. Pesisir batik took in Chinese, Arab and European influences and used reds, pinks and blues — [We Are Mandalas](https://wearemandalas.com/en-int/blogs/articles/batik-sogan) (commercial; consistent with Batik Guild)
- **Thaitone**: a colour scheme compiled by Pairoj Pittayamatee from about ten years of Silpakorn University thesis research on traditional Thai pigments. Lac gives red/purple-red, cinnabar gives *chard*, gamboge (*Garcinia hanburyi*) gives *rong* yellow, indigo gives *kraam* blue and malachite gives green. Five Benjarong primaries (chard, rong, kraam, black, white) are mixed into five more. Values are given in CMYK, so this is a designer's codification, not measured artefacts — [Wikipedia: Thaitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaitone)
- Benjarong ("five colours") porcelain usually has five basic mineral colours but in practice three to eight — [Wikipedia: Benjarong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjarong)
- Thai *khiaw* (green) has more than 30 traditional terms (e.g. *khiaw khor ped* "teal", *khiaw tang chae* "green bronze"). Their use is fading outside craftspeople and older speakers — [Thai Khadi Journal 2018](https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/thaikhadijournal/article/view/139079)

### Inferences
- The traditions share chemistry. Indigo underlies Andean, Indian, Javanese and Thai blues and Maya blue. Anthraquinone reds (cochineal, lac, chay/madder, morinda) underlie most reds. Iron underlies Falu red, Māori kōkōwai, Indian black and zellige brown. ChromaConscious could tag palette records by **colorant family** (indigoid, anthraquinone, iron oxide, iron-tannin, Cu/Co glaze). That is more accurate and less exoticising than tagging by nation.
- Hue is process-dependent. Tyrian purple shifts with pH and light; soga, morinda and chay shift with mordant; zellige green shifts with Cu vs Cr. A single hex per "traditional colour" is a simplification. Records should store a range or several swatches and say which process or period they stand for.
- Reflectance spectra (cochineal peaks around 520–560 nm, CHSOS FORS data, below) could be converted to CIELAB under D65 to get defensible sRGB approximations. That would be a derived value and should be labelled as such.
- CVD note (inference): kōwhaiwhai red/black/white and soga brown/indigo are mostly lightness-separated sets, so they are likely more CVD-robust than hue-separated ones. Zellige blue/green/white/black and Thaitone's red/green pairs need the existing CVD checks.

### Gaps
- No published CIELAB or sRGB values were found for cochineal Andean textiles, Maya blue, zellige glazes, kōkōwai, soga or Falu red paint. The Structural Chemistry Tyrian paper has CIELAB data, but I did not extract the numbers.
- Not fetched: Ajrakh (Sindh/Kutch; Pakistan/India), Bandhani, Holi and *gulal* pigments, Bangladeshi jamdani and Bengal indigo history (Jenny Balfour-Paul's *Indigo* was not consulted), turmeric (curcumin), saffron (crocin), henna (lawsone), kumkum/sindoor. All are relevant but unsourced here.
- Not fetched: Oaxacan cochineal/indigo/*caracol púrpura* (*Plicopurpura*) dyeing by present-day communities; Mexican *añil*.
- Not fetched: Pacific tapa/ngatu/siapo (Tonga/Samoa/Fiji) pigments; Hawaiian kapa.
- Not fetched: Arabic/Levantine textile dyes (e.g. Palestinian tatreez colours), Persian carpet dyes beyond the haft-rang tile work already covered; Mediterranean Greek/Italian earth pigments (e.g. terra di Siena), Portuguese azulejo cobalt.

## 2. Which regional colour "meanings" are well-supported, and which are folk claims?

### Takeaway
I found strong support only for **context-bound institutional meanings**: Māori red/black/white tied to cosmology and status, with documented Māori-authored and dictionary sources; Javanese court sogan versus coastal pesisir palettes; and royal or status uses of murex purple. Popular one-word glossaries of the form "colour X means Y in country Z" mostly trace to blogs. No empirical colour-emotion study for these regions turned up in this pass beyond the Arabian Gulf questionnaire below.

### Cited Findings
- **Māori (supported, institutional):** in the Tino Rangatiratanga flag, black = Te Korekore (potential being), red = Te Whai Ao (coming into being), white = Te Ao Mārama (the realm of light). The proverb "Mā whero, mā pango, ka oti te mahi" is usually read as chiefs (red) and commoners (black) working together — [Wikipedia: Tino Rangatiratanga flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tino_Rangatiratanga_flag); [He Kapunga Oneone](https://www.kauaeraro.com/onamata/ki-te-ao-marama-introduction-to-colour-theory-a-tirohanga-maori)
- **Māori (weakly sourced):** the claims that red ochre "represents Papatūānuku / the female principle" and that whero and kura are chiefly colours tied to mana and tapu come from a non-authoritative tattoo-symbol site. Treat them as plausible but unverified — [polynesiantattoosymbols.com](https://www.polynesiantattoosymbols.com/what-colors-are-used-in-Maori-art.html)
- **Javanese batik (supported as stylistic/social, not as fixed "meanings"):** sogan browns are linked to the inland courts and bright pesisir palettes to coastal, multi-ethnic trade towns — [Batik Guild](https://www.batikguild.org.uk/batik/indonesia); [We Are Mandalas](https://wearemandalas.com/en-int/blogs/articles/batik-sogan). Specific per-colour "meanings" lists on commercial batik blogs are folk-level — [Kasih Coop blog, example](https://www.kasihcoop.com/blogs/news/the-colors-of-indonesian-batik-meanings-behind-a-vibrant-heritage)
- **Benjarong and Lai nam thong:** the gold-leaf variant was mainly an aristocratic ware, which is a status link supported by history — [Wikipedia: Benjarong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjarong)
- **Arabian Gulf (measured, small sample):** 80 subjects rated 10 colours on 12 descriptors, and white was linked with clean, new, relaxed and focused — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/295397639_A_Cross-cultural_study_of_emotional_Responses_on_Colours)
- **Arab web (folk-adjacent):** an Arabic-interface paper states that white "symbolizes purity and peace" and that blue "some suggest stands for protection". These are asserted, not measured; only the usage frequencies were measured (see §3) — [arXiv 1203.3660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1203.3660)
- **Hue norms:** cultural differences in hue choice appear mainly where clear social norms prescribe colour use. Otherwise preferences converge, with blue most liked — summarised in [search results referencing a cross-cultural hue study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220108439_Colour_appeal_in_website_design_within_and_across_cultures_A_multi-method_evaluation) (exact source attribution uncertain; verify)
- **Brazil:** a 2025 study (n=40, mostly UFABC students) found blue the most preferred colour for both a mock store and a mock news site — [Displays/ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0141938225001830)

### Inferences
- **Tier "supported, context-bound"**: Māori red/black/white (cosmological and political, e.g. the Tino Rangatiratanga flag, which is an activist and political symbol, so using its palette in a product can read as a political statement); murex purple as historic elite or royal; Benjarong gold as aristocratic; sogan as court style.
- **Tier "folk / verify with audience"**: Indian "saffron = sacred, red = bridal, white = mourning"; Thai weekday colours; Islamic green; "Mexican colours = bright"; Nordic "minimalism"; Andean colour cosmologies. None were empirically sourced in this pass. Several have real ritual uses, but the evidence I gathered does not show they carry over to UI accent colours.
- Colour *usage* frequencies on websites (e.g. white backgrounds on Arab sites) are not evidence of colour *meaning*. The Arabic-interface paper mixes the two.

### Gaps
- No empirical colour-emotion study was fetched for India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Indonesia, Thailand, Mexico, Peru or Pacific nations. Jonauskaite 2020 includes some of these countries (e.g. India, Mexico); check its per-country data rather than relying on this pass.
- The Thai weekday-colour system (royal birth-day colours) and Indian ritual colours (sindoor, haldi, Holi) were not fetched.

## 3. What empirical web/app colour studies exist for these regions?

### Takeaway
The evidence is thin and mostly old. One recent large-sample colour-extraction study (Limniati & Giannakoulopoulos, about 2025) reports more colourful layouts in Mexico, India, Nicaragua, Malaysia and the UAE. Older work found Indian university sites *less* saturated than US ones (2003) and Arab sites dominated by white backgrounds with blue links. I found **no peer-reviewed study of the colour systems of regional super-apps** (Paytm, Careem, Gojek, Grab, M-Pesa, Mercado Libre).

### Cited Findings
- **Limniati & Giannakoulopoulos (Ionian University), "Insights Regarding the Cultural Identity of Different Countries on the Web through Aesthetic Analysis":** systematic extraction of primary and secondary colours, schemes and backgrounds from popular sites worldwide, with statistical analysis. Designers in Mexico, India, Nicaragua, Malaysia and the UAE "chose to use more colorful layouts probably in accordance with the local perception of aesthetics". The causal part ("probably") is the authors' speculation — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394843823_Insights_Regarding_the_Cultural_Identity_of_Different_Countries_on_the_Web_through_Aesthetic_Analysis)
- **Arabic interfaces (cultural-markers study):** white was used as the page background on 85% of sites and as menu font colour on 48%. Blue was used for links (55%) and menu backgrounds (26%) — [arXiv 1203.3660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1203.3660)
- **Arab vs US university homepages** (Algiers, Cairo, KFUPM vs Harvard, Stanford, Columbia): white was the most common background in both groups. The study is framed with Hofstede — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328748786)
- **India vs US university sites (Rajkumar 2003):** US images were high-quality and saturated; Indian images were washed out, duotone and low-saturation. This likely reflects bandwidth and production constraints of the time more than taste (my inference) — reported via [Callahan 2005, JCMC](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2006.tb00312.x) and search summary
- **"Cultural Analysis of Indian Websites"** lists language, pictures, symbols and colour as critical factors and proposes an "Integration" dimension beyond Hofstede's. This is qualitative — [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/45321292/Cultural_Analysis_of_Indian_Websites)
- **China/India/Japan/US content analysis:** local sites reflect home-country values and argue against a "transnational web style" — [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/3354784)
- **"Digital palettes" (15 countries):** some colours are used universally, and culture-specific and industry-specific palettes can be identified — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/279810550_Digital_palettes_assessing_cultural_and_industry_specific_color_usage_in_website_design)
- **Brazil:** an internet-banking experiment (n=110, 22 per region) found regional VSM cultural differences that tracked UX ratings, especially for amount of information and colour — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/283594654). Blue was preferred in a 2025 mock-site study (n=40) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0141938225001830)
- **Mexico:** Singh & Baack 2004 (95 Mexican sites) found cultural adaptation. Sachau & Hutchinson 2012 found more Hofstede-linked "culturally relevant" features on Mexican government sites. Neither measured saturation — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220437869); [Springer ETR&D](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-012-9270-5)
- The claim that "Mexicans prefer more colourful screens" is a generalisation repeated across the literature, not a measurement — per the same search; compare [Marcus 2001](https://users.jyu.fi/~japawlow/05_marcus2001.pdf) (already cited in prior notes)
- **Saturation vs trust:** for 50 sites in 10 domains, high saturation can *lower* trustworthiness and appeal, depending on domain (not region-specific) — [Computers in Human Behavior 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563216302254)

### Inferences
- Two measured data points can look contradictory: India's colourful layouts (about 2025) versus low-saturation Indian images (2003). They measure different things (layout colourfulness vs photo saturation) at different times. Neither supports a fixed "Indian = vivid" default. The safer encoding is that colourfulness is an audience parameter, which matches the prior notes on Reinecke & Gajos.
- White backgrounds with blue links on Arab sites fit the global default and are not a distinct regional norm. For Arabic, RTL layout and script legibility matter more than hue (outside this scope).
- A ChromaConscious agent should not choose hue from the region. At most it could nudge the colourfulness range, and only with a citation and an explicit user override.

### Gaps
- **Super-apps:** no peer-reviewed or measured analysis found of Paytm, PhonePe, Careem, Gojek, Grab, M-Pesa, Mercado Libre or LINE brand and UI palettes. Observations such as "SEA super-apps converge on green" would be my own reading of brand assets and are not sourced here.
- No studies found for Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Peru, Bolivia, Nordic-specific or Pacific Island web colour. A 2023–2026 mobile or dark-mode cross-cultural study was not found.
- I could not access the full Limniati & Giannakoulopoulos text (ResearchGate). Sample size, year and metrics are unverified.

## 4. Respectful use, and which open datasets exist (with licences)?

### Takeaway
Three legal or normative anchors are concrete and citable. (1) **Mexico's 2022 Federal Law** protecting Indigenous and Afro-Mexican cultural heritage gives communities control over their designs and imposes penalties. (2) **Te Mana Raraunga's Māori Data Sovereignty principles** treat data *about* Māori culture, including derived colour data, as subject to Māori authority. (3) **Te Papa's Mana Taonga** practice puts taonga images behind iwi consultation. Open spectral data exists (CHSOS), but its licence is unstated.

### Cited Findings

**Mexico / Mesoamerica**
- Ley Federal de Protección del Patrimonio Cultural de los Pueblos y Comunidades Indígenas y Afromexicanas (DOF 17 Jan 2022). Communities decide which expressions are off-limits and which may be used with prior consent. It requires fair benefit-sharing and sets up a registry. Copying "even to a degree of confusion" is actionable, with up to 10 years' prison for illegal production or sale — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Law_for_the_Protection_of_Cultural_Heritage_of_Indigenous_and_Afro-Mexican_Peoples_and_Communities); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/mexico/indigenous-peoples/1343422/protecting-the-cultural-heritage-of-indigenous-and-afro-mexican-peoples); [Center for Art Law](https://itsartlaw.org/art-law/innovations-in-protecting-art-and-design-placing-mexicos-recent-ban-on-cultural-appropriation-in-greater-context/)
- Cases: the Mixe community of Santa María Tlahuitoltepec against Isabel Marant (2015, 2020) and Anthropologie (2021); about 300 Oaxacan artisans against J. Marie and Tuckernuck. Enforcement abroad is weak: Shein received only a warning — [Center for Art Law](https://itsartlaw.org/art-law/innovations-in-protecting-art-and-design-placing-mexicos-recent-ban-on-cultural-appropriation-in-greater-context/); [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/culture/oaxaca-artisans-accuse-u-s-brands-of-cultural-appropriation-traditional-designs/); [Härting](https://haerting.de/en/insights/law-against-cultural-appropriation/); [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/mexico-testing-limits-of-using-law-to-bar-cultural-appropriation)

**Māori / Aotearoa**
- Te Mana Raraunga defines Māori data as "digital or digitisable information or knowledge that is about or from Māori people, language, culture, resources or environments". The principles (Oct 2018) include Rangatiratanga (control, jurisdiction, self-determination), Whakapapa (context, future harm), Kotahitanga (collective benefit) and Kaitiakitanga. Under Kaitiakitanga, Māori decide which data is controlled (tapu) or open (noa). The data should preferably be stored in Aotearoa — [TMR principles PDF (Otago)](https://www.otago.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0014/321044/tmr-maori-data-sovereignty-principles-october-2018-832194.pdf); [Te Mana Raraunga](https://www.temanararaunga.maori.nz/); [Waikato library guide](https://libraryguides.waikato.ac.nz/DataSov/matapono). Note: the search tool's summary filled in some principle headings (Whanaungatanga, Manaakitanga) from model knowledge; confirm them against the PDF.
- Karaitiana Taiuru argues that the TMR principles need revision for new technologies. The Waitangi Tribunal's WAI 2522 report affirms that Māori data is a taonga — [Taiuru](https://www.taiuru.co.nz/critical-analysis-mana-raraunga/)
- AUT links TMR to the international CARE principles for Indigenous data governance — [AUT LibGuide](https://aut.ac.nz.libguides.com/c.php?g=982265&p=7157663)
- Te Papa does not release taonga images without consulting the originating whānau, hapū or iwi (Mana Taonga), and some are withheld online. Collections API data is ODC-By 1.0. Images may be "No Known Copyright Restrictions", CC BY 4.0 (mainly science specimens), CC BY-NC-ND or All Rights Reserved. A request to treat taonga and tūpuna images respectfully is "best practice", outside the formal terms. JSTOR's 45k Te Papa open images excluded taonga and redirected to Te Papa — [Te Papa: Copyright & terms](https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/about/collections/all-te-papa-websites/copyright-and-terms-use); [Te Papa API terms PDF](https://www.tepapa.govt.nz/assets/76067/1693184837-collections_api_terms_of_use.pdf); [JSTOR](https://about.jstor.org/blog/new-45000-images-from-museum-of-new-zealand-te-papa-tongarewa/)

### Inferences (design recommendations for ChromaConscious; not sourced claims)
- **Ship colours, not designs.** The Mexican law covers "symbols, designs or other elements" copied to the point of confusion. A flat swatch set is far from that line, but a bundled huipil, kōwhaiwhai, tā moko or batik motif, or a name like "Tlahuitoltepec blouse palette", moves toward it. Do not ship motifs, and do not name palettes after specific communities without consent.
- **Treat derived Māori colour data as Māori data.** Under TMR's definition, a "kōwhaiwhai palette" derived from photos of taonga is plausibly Māori data. The respectful default is a materials-level record (kōkōwai red ochre, soot black, natural wood or white), with sources at Te Ara and Te Aka and a pointer to the protocols, and no colours sampled from specific taonga images. The Tino Rangatiratanga flag colours carry political meaning; flag that, don't offer them as a "Māori theme".
- **Respect trademarks.** Call a Falu-inspired swatch "iron-oxide red (rödfärg type)", not "Falu Rödfärg". Do not ship NCS atlas values. Hering-opponent ideas are fine to cite.
- **Name by material and process**, e.g. "cochineal on camelid (Wari-period, approx.)", "copper-green zellige glaze (pre-modern)", "sogan bark brown", "lac red", "murex purple, debrominated variant". Store period and process, because the chemistry shows the hue depends on both.
- Any spectra-derived values need a licence check before inclusion. CHSOS data has no stated licence, so link to it rather than redistributing.
