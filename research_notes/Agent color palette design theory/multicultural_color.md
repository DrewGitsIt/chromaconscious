Many hex values online come from blogs and conflict with each other (see below). Traditions I could not source in this pass are listed under Gaps, not filled from memory.

## 1. What is universal vs. culture-specific in color-emotion association and color preference?

### Takeaway
Color-*emotion* associations are mostly shared worldwide (r = .88 across 30 nations), with measurable local tuning that tracks language, geography and mourning or religious customs. Color *preference* (which hues people like) is much less universal. The "everyone likes blue" result holds in industrialized samples but fails in at least one non-industrialized group (the Himba). The most robust universals are the low-level dimensions (warm/cool, heavy/light, active/passive) and the warm-vs-cool communication asymmetry. Like/dislike and "harmony" judgments vary more.

### Cited Findings
**Color-emotion (Jonauskaite et al.)**
- 4,598 participants in 30 nations (22 languages) linked 20 emotion concepts to 12 color *terms*. Mean pattern similarity between nations was r = .88. Even so, a classifier could identify a participant's nation with 34.4% accuracy, and nations closer in language or geography were more similar. Psychological Science 31(10):1245–1260, DOI 10.1177/0956797620948810 — [Colour Experience lab news](https://www.colourexperience.ch/news/2020/9/15/new-article); [HAL open copy](https://hal.science/hal-03646867v1)
- Red is the only color strongly linked to both a positive emotion (love) and a negative one (anger). Red and black were rated the most emotional colors, brown the least — [PsychNewsDaily summary](https://psychnewsdaily.com/colors-and-the-emotions-people-associate-them-with-are-similar-around-the-world/)
- 14 color-emotion pairings were reported in almost all countries. Nation-specific deviations: Nigerians also linked red with fear; Chinese also linked white with sadness; Greeks linked purple with sadness while other nations linked it with positive emotions; Egyptians did not link yellow with joy. The authors tie the white and purple cases to mourning customs (white worn at Chinese funerals; dark purple in Greek Orthodox mourning periods) — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200910150247.htm)
- Earlier 4-nation study (n = 711; China, Germany, Greece, UK; Royal Society Open Science 2019): only Greeks strongly linked PURPLE with sadness. China linked WHITE with negative emotions more often than the other three. BROWN–disgust was strongest in Germany and almost absent in China — [Jonauskaite et al. 2019, R. Soc. Open Sci.](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/abs/10.1098/rsos.190741)
- In Western cultures white is mostly positive. In China it also carries "sadness", which makes white more ambiguous there (implicit-association follow-up) — [PMC10017663](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10017663/)
- Limitation: like many cross-cultural studies, the samples are not representative (mostly university or online samples) — [Psychology Today commentary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/culture-conscious/202011/do-people-everywhere-feel-blue-and-turn-green-with-envy)
- A third-party reference database, the Global Color Meaning Database (founded by Chloe Chen, CC BY 4.0, last updated Sept 2026), grades this study "A" and uses it as a baseline: "shared tendency first, local tuning second." It has letter-graded evidence records and may be useful as a structured source. I only verified one record — [GCMD-C01](https://www.globalcolormeaning.org/entries/gcmd-c01.html)

**Color emotion and harmony (Ou, Luo et al.)**
- Two-color combinations rated by about 223 observers in the UK, Taiwan, France, Germany, Spain, Sweden, Argentina and Iran. Warm/cool, heavy/light and active/passive were consistent across cultures. **Like/dislike was not**: Argentinian observers differed significantly. Gender, age and professional background also mattered. Color Res. Appl. 37(1):23–43 (2012) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230219167_A_Cross-Cultural_Comparison_of_Colour_Emotion_for_Two-Colour_Combinations)
- "Universal models of colour emotion and colour harmony" pooled data from 12 regions (Argentina, China, France, Germany, Hungary, Iran, Japan, Spain, Sweden, Taiwan, Thailand, UK). Average model correlations: warm/cool 0.78, heavy/light 0.80, active/passive 0.81, harmony 0.72. Color Res. Appl. 43(5):736–748 (2018) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/col.22243)
- An earlier six-country harmony study (Britain, China, France, Germany, Spain, Sweden; DRS 2004) found little cultural effect on harmony — [DRS Digital Library](https://dl.designresearchsociety.org/drs-conference-papers/drs2004/researchpapers/60/)

**Color preference**
- Hurlbert & Ling 2007 (Current Biology 17(16)): 171 British and 37 Han Chinese living in the UK, 8 hues. Women shifted toward reddish/purple and men toward blue-green. The Chinese participants preferred reddish colors more overall, which the authors read as a cultural effect — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096098220701559X)
- Replications and critiques: Al-Rasheed (Arabic vs British) and a Frontiers reanalysis found sex differences correlate across cultures, but the "biological L-M component" claim held only for the Chinese observers. Sorokowski et al. argued the two-component analysis is close to circular with so few equiluminant hues, and noted the original Chinese sample lived in the UK — [Frontiers 2015 commentary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01840/full); [Sorokowski et al. 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-014-0591-8)
- Bonnardel et al. 2018 (Indian sample): a similar sex-difference pattern plus a culture-specific contribution, mostly in women — [Wiley Color Res. Appl.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/col.22188)
- Hadza hunter-gatherers (Tanzania): women and men had *similar* color preferences — [Groyecka et al. 2019, Perception](https://dx.doi.org/10.1177/0301006619840937)
- Ecological Valence Theory (Palmer & Schloss 2010): people like colors to the degree they like the objects associated with them. Object valence explained about 80% of variance in Americans' preferences for 32 colors (blues = sky/water liked; browns/olive/chartreuse = feces/rot disliked) — [Taylor & Franklin 2012, PB&R](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-012-0222-1)
- **Taylor, Clifford & Franklin 2013, "Color preferences are not universal"** (J. Exp. Psychol. Gen. 142:1015–1027): Himba (rural Namibia) preferences had little in common with British ones. The Himba rated "universal" blues low, rated saturated red/orange/yellow higher, and showed no (or reversed) sex differences — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233410106_Color_Preferences_Are_Not_Universal); [PDF](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/Taylor%20Schloss%20Palmer%20Franklin.pdf)
- EVT fits US/UK data well, fits Japanese and Arabic data less well, and does not fit Himba data — [Yokosawa et al. 2016, Cognitive Science (US vs Japan)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cogs.12291); [Al-Rasheed et al. 2022 Arabic EVT test](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366500366_A_test_of_the_ecological_valence_theory_of_color_preference_the_case_of_Arabic)
- Epicoco, Mohr & Jonauskaite (2026, QJEP): favourite and least-favourite colors "tell different stories". Preference draws on abstract concepts and personal meaning, not only object valence — [SAGE](https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218261421580)

**Color naming (Berlin & Kay lineage / World Color Survey)**
- Gibson et al. 2017 (PNAS 114(40)) analyzed WCS data from 110 languages. In every language, warm colors (reds/yellows) are communicated more efficiently than cool colors (blues/greens). Proposed reason: salient foreground objects tend to be warm, backgrounds cool. Variation in efficiency across languages points to a cultural role too — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1619666114)
- Zaslavsky et al. 2019 reproduced the warm advantage but attributed it to perceptual structure, not communicative need — [Wiley TopiCS](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/tops.12395); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.06165)
- Follow-up (Cognition): as languages gain color-communication efficiency, gains come first for yellow, then brown, then purple — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027719302604)

### Inferences
- For an agent, the ranking of evidence strength is roughly: perceptual dimensions (warm/cool, heavy/light, active/passive) > broad emotion associations (red = high arousal/love+anger, black = sadness, etc.) > hue *preference* > specific symbolic "meanings". Preference-based defaults ("users like blue") are the weakest and the most Western-sample-biased.
- Most well-documented local deviations involve **mourning and religion** (white in China, purple in Greece). These are low-cost to encode as flags ("white-dominant + somber context may read as mourning in Chinese audiences") and do not require stereotyping a whole palette.
- Nearly all of this research used color *terms* or a few monitor chips, not real UI compositions. Applying it to interface palettes is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- No study I found tests color-emotion for actual UI palettes (multi-role themes) across cultures.
- Representation is uneven. Sub-Saharan Africa (beyond Nigeria and Himba/Hadza), South Asia, Southeast Asia, the Pacific and Indigenous American groups are thinly covered.
- I did not fetch Berlin & Kay 1969 or Kay et al.'s WCS volume directly. This section relies on Gibson 2017's use of the WCS.

## 2. Concrete named color traditions / palette systems usable as grounded references

### Takeaway
Several traditions have **authoritative, structured color data**: Japan (JIS Z 8102, 269 names defined in Munsell), China (the Palace Museum's 384-name *Chinese Traditional Colors*, season-ordered and tied to artifacts) and Korea (obangsaek's 5 + obangansaek intermediate colors, the Korean Color Atlas). Most other traditions are documented by **materials and practice** (pigments, dyes, glazes) rather than standard color values. Hex codes for them online are mostly modern reinterpretations that disagree with each other. An agent-facing reference should store provenance (source type, material, Munsell/Lab if known) and say when a value is approximate.

### Cited Findings
**Japan**
- JIS Z 8102 ("Names of non-luminous object colours"), current edition 2001, replaced 1985. It defines 269 colors in **Munsell notation**, not hex (e.g. *azuki-iro* = 8R 4.5/4.5). JIS "red" converts to about #BE0032, not #FF0000. RGB conversions: the R package *Nippon* and color-site.com — [Nippon (CRAN)](https://rdrr.io/cran/Nippon/man/JapaneseColors.html); [color-site JIS list](https://en.color-site.com/jis_colors); [GlobalSpec JIS Z 8102](https://standards.globalspec.com/std/1207234/JIS%20Z%208102)
- *Kasane no irome* (Heian period, 794–1185): seasonal multi-layer garment combinations. Wearing the wrong season's combination was considered vulgar. Examples (outer/middle/lining): Ume-gasane (early spring) red plum / white / peach; Kakitsubata-gasane (summer) iris / dayflower / young grass; Momiji-gasane (autumn) maple / rose / Rikyū brown; Yuki-gasane (winter) snow / sky / indigo grey. That site says its values come from Wada Sanzō's *Nihon Shiki* and DIC/PANTONE traditional guides, adjusted to sRGB — [getzenquery Japanese traditional colors](https://www.getzenquery.com/tools/japanese-traditional-colors/) (secondary source; treat as approximate)
- **Hex disagreement example:** *sakura-iro* is #FEEEED on ColorFYI and #FCC9B9 on Coloracci. ColorFYI also gives ai-iro (indigo) #004C71, kitsune-iro #C3803A, matcha #BEC23F, sumi #1C1C1C — [ColorFYI](https://colorfyi.com/blog/japanese-traditional-colors/); [Coloracci](https://coloracci.ai/blog/japanese-color-culture-traditions)
- Kasane combinations are *not* part of JIS. For them, use Heian-record-based color dictionaries — same sources as above.

**China**
- *中国传统色：故宫里的色彩美学 / Chinese Traditional Colors: The Aesthetics of Colors in the Forbidden City* (Palace Museum). It organizes **384 traditional color names**, each matched to Palace Museum artifacts and ordered by season. Names include mineral pigments such as Cinnabar (朱砂), Azurite (石青), Second Green (二绿), Verdigris and Malachite Green — [Baidu Baike entry](https://baike.baidu.com/en/item/Chinese%20Traditional%20Colors:%20The%20Aesthetics%20of%20Colors%20in%20the%20Forbidden%20City/71682)
- Chinese painting pigments fall into two classes: mineral "stone colours" (石色: cinnabar/vermilion, malachite green 石绿, azurite blue 石青, realgar orange, ochre, orpiment yellow 石黄) and plant "water colours" (水色, e.g. indigo). Mineral pigments are graded by grind size: coarse = deeper, fine = paler. This is the origin of numbered names like 头绿/二绿/三绿. Painters lay translucent plant color under mineral color — [HKU Museum Chinese painting pigments](https://mmea.hku.hk/chinese-painting-pigments/); [Wikipedia: Chinese pigment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_pigment)
- Cinnabar was used as pigment by the Yangshao culture (c. 5000–4000 BC). Terracotta Army pigments include cinnabar, azurite, malachite, bone/lead white and synthetic "Han purple" — [Wikipedia: Vermilion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermilion); [Wikipedia: Chinese pigment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_pigment)
- Possible hex dataset: colorarchive.org "China (Traditional)" region palette — [colorarchive](https://colorarchive.org/regions/china-traditional/) (not verified; provenance unclear)

**Korea**
- *Obangsaek* (오방색, "five-direction colors"): blue/cheong = east/wood/spring; red/jeok = south/fire/summer; yellow/hwang = centre/earth; white/baek = west/metal/autumn; black/heuk = north/water/winter. Intermediate colors are *obanganseak/ogansaek* (오간색) — [Wikipedia: Obangsaek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obangsaek)
- *Dancheong* (단청, literally "cinnabar and blue-green"): painted decoration of temples, palaces and Confucian buildings in the five-color system, layered into 20+ secondary hues. Its richness signals the building's rank. It also protects the wood. It still appears in contemporary posters, fashion and event graphics — [Wikipedia: Dancheong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dancheong); [Korean Air Morning Calm 2026](https://morningcalm.koreanair.com/en/issues/march-april-2026-vol-50-no-02/dancheong)
- The Korean Color Atlas lists traditional colors with sources (dancheong pigments, Joseon dyeing). It is a candidate structured dataset — [koreancoloratlas.com](https://koreancoloratlas.com/) (not verified in depth)
- The Chinese five-element color scheme (wuxing) uses the same element/direction/color mapping as obangsaek — [Wikipedia: Obangsaek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obangsaek)

**Persia / Iran (Islamic tilework)**
- *Haft rang* ("seven colours"): overglaze polychrome tiles fired in a single firing, with black lines separating colors. Widely used on Safavid façades (Isfahan, Qazvin, Mashhad) in the 17th century. Main colors: black, white, turquoise, yellow, rose pink, cobalt blue. The name does not mean all seven appear on each tile. Single firing makes the colors less intense than in mosaic (*mo'araq*) tilework — [Wikipedia: Haft-rang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haft-rang)
- Spectroscopy of 17th-century tiles found more than 250 glazes across white, brown, yellow, black, green, turquoise, blue and violet — [ScienceDirect, J. Archaeological Science 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305440313003245)
- Turquoise comes from copper oxide (about 3.5 wt% CuO in a 19th-century Tehran glaze). Deep "lajvard" blue is normally cobalt, though lapis lazuli was found in one 13th-century glaze — [Royal Society Notes & Records](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsnr/article/75/1/5/48705/We-must-send-you-a-sample-a-Persian-European); [Wikipedia: Lajvardina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lajvardina)
- Blog hex values (Firoozeh #00CED1, Lajvard #0047AB, "Shah Mosque Turquoise" #0097A7) are modern interpretations, not measured — [Coloracci Persian colors](https://coloracci.ai/blog/persian-iranian-colors)

**West Africa**
- *Bògòlanfini* (Bamana, Mali): men weave the cotton and women apply the design. The cloth is soaked in a tannin-rich leaf bath (n'gallama, *Anogeissus leiocarpus*, and *Combretum glutinosum*), which turns it yellow. Iron-rich fermented river mud painted on reacts with the tannins to make black. Unpainted areas are bleached back toward cream. The resulting palette is roughly yellow-ochre/brown, black and off-white — [Bowers Museum](https://collections.bowers.org/objects/144806/mud-cloth-bogolanfini?ctx=a511dcd296f5085a2c8c5dedfd9ddca227aa8d94&idx=4); [Wikipedia: Bògòlanfini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%B2g%C3%B2lanfini); [Saint Louis Art Museum](https://www.slam.org/collection/objects/54270/)
- *Kente* (Asante, Ghana): originally royal cloth for the Asantehene. Orange (royalty/wealth) is described as the original color for the king's cloth — [Miami University Art Museum](https://sites.miamioh.edu/art-museum/2019/02/kente-cloth-a-look-inside-40-at-40/). Historic dyes: indigo (blue), camwood (red), tamarind (brown) — [WebExhibits](https://www.webexhibits.org/colorart/african-kente.html)
- The widely repeated kente color key (gold = status, green = renewal, blue = harmony, black = ancestors...) is simplified. Meanings are "contextual, historically layered, and sometimes contradictory" across weavers, and much of the meaning lives in named patterns and proverbs, not color alone — [Craft Atlas: Kente](https://craftatlas.co/crafts/kente) (secondary)

**Aboriginal Australia**
- The traditional ochre palette is red, yellow, white and black, from iron oxides, charcoal, kaolin/gypsum ("pipe clay") and shell. Ochres are traded over long distances, collected from specific, often sacred sites, and associated with Ancestral Beings. **Meanings vary by Country (Nation)**. There is no single pan-Aboriginal color code — [Mandel Art Gallery](https://mandelartgallery.com.au/blogs/mandel-art-gallery-blog/aboriginal-ochre-colours); [Wikipedia: Ochre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre) (secondary/commercial gallery)

**Mexico**
- Luis Barragán's Casa Gilardi (1975–77): bright pink front and rear façades, light purple eastern boundary wall, white auxiliary volumes, yellow glass at the pool, and pink/blue/yellow interior walls. He left green to the vegetation. He chose final hues on site under real light, drew on Mexican folk sources such as painter Chucho Reyes, and left mixing instructions for his paints — [ArchEyes](https://archeyes.com/casa-gilardi-a-masterpiece-of-color-and-light-by-luis-barragan/); [Dwell](https://www.dwell.com/article/luis-barragan-casa-gilardi-cuadra-san-cristobal-8dde56ff)

### Inferences
- For ChromaConscious, the most defensible references are **material-grounded** ones: a pigment or dye with known chemistry (cinnabar HgS, malachite, azurite, copper turquoise, cobalt blue, iron-tannin black, red/yellow ochre, indigo), combined with a sourced standard where one exists (JIS Munsell values, Palace Museum names). They make better "seeds" than invented symbolic meanings.
- Several traditions come with **structural** rules that map naturally onto a theme engine:
  - Kasane: ordered three-layer seasonal combinations, which suit surface/elevation layers.
  - Obangsaek/wuxing: 5 primaries plus intermediates, which suit a 5-role categorical set.
  - Haft rang: colors on a white ground separated by black lines, i.e. a high-contrast outline system.
  - Bògòlan: 3-tone tannin/iron palette, a natural low-chroma theme.
  - Barragán: saturated planes with nature as the neutral.
- Many of these palettes include **red/green or pink/turquoise pairs at similar lightness**. They need the existing CVD checks (see docs/colorblind-spec.md) applied; cultural authenticity should not override legibility.
- A traditional pigment value describes how a pigment looks on cloth, wood or glaze under daylight. It is not a screen color. Any sRGB value is an interpretation and should be labeled that way.

### Gaps (not sourced in this pass; do not fill from memory)
- India (Holi, rangoli, regional textiles such as Bandhani/Ajrakh, temple palettes) — no sources fetched.
- Andean textiles (cochineal, Paracas/Inca), Oaxacan textiles, papel picado — not fetched.
- Nordic/Scandinavian, Mediterranean, Maghreb (e.g. Majorelle blue, zellige), Pacific Islands (tapa/ngatu) — not fetched.
- Primary sources for Lab/Munsell measurements outside Japan. The Chinese 384-name book's actual CMYK/RGB values were not verified. Korean Color Atlas provenance not verified.
- Kassia St Clair (*The Secret Lives of Colour*) and Pastoureau (*Blue*, *Black*, *Red*, *Green*, *Yellow*) were not consulted directly. Note that Pastoureau's histories are largely European.

## 3. Cultural differences in UI/web color norms

### Takeaway
Measured data does **not** support the stereotype that East Asian sites are more saturated. Several studies find Japanese (and some Chinese) sites have *high information density but low average saturation*, and US/Anglo sites higher saturation. The most reliable large-sample finding (Reinecke & Gajos, about 40k participants) is that the preferred levels of colorfulness and complexity differ by country, gender and age, which argues against one universal "good taste" default. Hofstede-based frameworks (Marcus & Gould) are influential, but their predictions often fail against measured data.

### Cited Findings
- Reinecke & Gajos, CHI 2014: about 2.4M appeal ratings from about 40k LabintheWild participants on 430 websites, with colorfulness and complexity computed by image metrics. The level at which appeal peaks varies by group. Women liked more colorful sites than men; Russians preferred lower complexity; Macedonians liked highly colorful designs more than any other country. The authors question universal design guidelines. The dataset is public. About 43% of participants were from the US — [PDF](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2014/reinecke14visual.pdf); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2556288.2557052); [data](https://www.labinthewild.org/data)
- Nordhoff et al. (cited in a CHI 2021 eye-tracking paper): Japanese sites tend to have high visual complexity and low average saturation; US sites medium complexity and highly saturated colors — [ACM CHI 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3411764.3445519)
- Li, Karreman & de Jong (IEEE ProComm 2022) reviewed 17 Chinese-vs-Western website studies. Firm color conclusions: Chinese sites use more red and blue, Western sites more white. Most studies were hard to compare, and Hofstede/Hall-based explanations were sometimes questionable — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/ProComm53155.2022.00023)
- In a cross-national comparison of measured site features, theory predicted high color and saturation for Japan but data did not match. Highest saturation was in Chile, then Nigeria. Lowest was Saudi Arabia. Japan had the highest information density — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348017616_Culturally_Sensitive_Website_Elements_and_Features_A_Cross-National_Comparison_of_Websites_from_Selected_Countries)
- PLOS ONE (urban multimedia portals) surveyed Japanese, UK and Chinese students. The Japanese gave white/red backgrounds a very low score (0.06) and disliked red. UK and Chinese respondents rated red higher. All three rated white/green combinations relatively high — [PLOS ONE 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0282712)
- Chinese typefaces lack true bold/italic variants, so Chinese UIs often use color, saturation and contrast for emphasis. This produces locally strong color even when average saturation is low (practitioner claim, not measured) — [Medium, Leo Geng](https://medium.com/@leogeng.slgz/adapting-ui-ux-across-cultures-f2e34eb039e1)
- Marcus & Gould 2000 (ACM *Interactions* 7(4):32–46) mapped Hofstede's five dimensions onto web UI. Their color examples: Judeo-Christian sacred colors (red, blue, white, gold) vs Buddhist saffron vs Islamic green, and whether subdued Finnish designs would suit the Mediterranean or Bollywood. About 600 citations; related Malaysia-vs-US comparison by Gould, Zakaria & Yusof — [ACM via Mendeley](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/ef6ae41e-3574-3785-ae1f-7dcf60ead13e/); [Marcus 2001 PDF](https://users.jyu.fi/~japawlow/05_marcus2001.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Low-saturation, lots of white, a single blue accent" is a measurable *regional* (Anglo/US-tech) pattern, not a universal optimum. Even within it, US sites are measured as more saturated than Japanese ones, so "minimal" and "saturated" are separate axes.
- Agents should treat **density/complexity** and **colorfulness** as separate audience parameters, and set them from audience evidence (or user choice) rather than from Hofstede-score lookups, which have weak predictive validity for color.
- Treating "East Asia" as one category hides real differences (Japan disliking red backgrounds, China favoring red and blue).

### Gaps
- No good measured studies found for Arab (beyond the single Saudi data point), Indian, African (beyond Nigeria) or Latin American (beyond Chile/Argentina) web color norms. Korea was barely covered.
- Most studies predate the dark-mode and mobile-super-app era. Nothing found from 2023–2026 measuring cross-cultural *app* palettes.

## 4. Cultural color taboos and connotations — what is supported vs. folk

### Takeaway
Two connotations are directly supported by cross-national data: **white with mourning/sadness in China** and **purple with mourning in Greece (Orthodox liturgy)**. Red's dual valence (love and anger) is a universal, and in Nigeria red also carries fear. Most of the common "X means Y in country Z" infographic claims could not be traced to empirical sources in this pass. They should be treated as folk lore until sourced, and an agent should present them as *possible* sensitivities, not rules.

### Cited Findings
- White and sadness/negative emotion in China, tied to white funeral clothing — [ScienceDaily on Jonauskaite 2020](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200910150247.htm); [Jonauskaite 2019](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/abs/10.1098/rsos.190741)
- Purple and sadness in Greece, tied to Orthodox liturgical purple in Advent, Lent and mourning — [Jonauskaite 2019](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/abs/10.1098/rsos.190741)
- Red: universally both love and anger. Nigerians additionally linked it with fear. Egyptians did not link yellow with joy — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200910150247.htm)
- Chinese participants preferred reddish hues more than British participants did, which is consistent with red's positive cultural role (the luck link is the critics' interpretation, not measured) — [Hurlbert & Ling 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096098220701559X); [critique blog](https://dsowen.wordpress.com/2011/12/15/week-12-topic-sex/)
- Japanese respondents disliked red/white background combinations for web portals — [PLOS ONE 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0282712)
- Religious color associations (Buddhist saffron, Islamic green, Judeo-Christian red/blue/white/gold) appear as design-guidance examples in Marcus & Gould, not as measured findings — [Marcus 2001](https://users.jyu.fi/~japawlow/05_marcus2001.pdf)
- Kente color "meanings" are contextual and contradictory across weavers. Aboriginal ochre meanings vary by Nation. Fixed one-word color glossaries for these cultures are oversimplifications — [Craft Atlas](https://craftatlas.co/crafts/kente); [Mandel Art Gallery](https://mandelartgallery.com.au/blogs/mandel-art-gallery-blog/aboriginal-ochre-colours)

### Inferences
- **Poorly supported or untested in what I found** (flag, don't encode as fact): "purple = mourning in Brazil/Thailand", "yellow = mourning in Egypt" (the data shows only a missing joy association, not mourning), "green is forbidden/sacred in Islamic contexts so avoid it", "red = danger is universal", "blue = trust universally", and fixed one-word meanings for kente or ochre colors. Some of these may be true in specific contexts. I found no empirical source, so they belong in a "possible sensitivity, verify with audience" tier.
- Practical encoding: (a) universal tier (perceptual dimensions, broad emotion map); (b) supported cultural deviation tier, with citation; (c) folk/unverified tier, shown as a soft caution only.

### Gaps
- No empirical source found for the commonly cited Brazil/Thailand purple mourning, Islamic green taboos in UI, or the universality of red as warning. A targeted search is needed (e.g. ISO 3864 safety colors vs cultural studies).
- Context matters (a funeral-adjacent product vs a fintech dashboard), but no study tests how strongly symbolic connotations carry over to interface accent colors.

## 5. Risks: appropriation, exoticizing, respectful presentation

### Takeaway
A flat color set is generally low-risk. The real risks are copying **motifs, patterns, sacred designs and names** without consent, and presenting a culture as one fixed "exotic" palette. The Australian ICIP framework is a concrete, citable model: provenance, consent, no sacred material, benefit-sharing. Palettes should also carry context and an honest statement of how approximate they are.

### Cited Findings
- Australian copyright protects only individual Indigenous artists. Communally owned cultural IP has no specific legal protection. A Productivity Commission draft found about 2 in 3 "Indigenous-style" products sold in Australia are fake, and recommended mandatory disclosure — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/labelling-fake-art-isnt-enough-australia-needs-to-recognise-and-protect-first-nations-cultural-and-intellectual-property-187426)
- ICIP practice (Arts Law Centre of Australia): get community consent, use only as consented, exclude secret/sacred material, avoid offensive use, share benefits — [Arts Law Centre](https://www.artslaw.com.au/information-sheet/indigenous-cultural-intellectual-property-icip-aitb/)
- Creative Australia's *Protocols for using First Nations Cultural and Intellectual Property in the Arts* (Terri Janke) and WIPO coverage of the protocols — [WIPO Magazine 2020](https://www.wipo.int/wipo_magazine/en/2020/04/article_0006.html); [Terri Janke](https://www.terrijanke.com.au/post/spotlight-on-first-nations-fashion-and-intellectual-property)
- Canadian *Indigenous Protocols for the Visual Arts* (CARFAC/IIP, 2025) — [PDF](https://indigenous-protocols-test-site.squarespace.com/s/CARFAC-IIP-DOCUMENT_DIGITAL_ENGLISH_2025.pdf)
- Case: Bibi Barba's Aboriginal geometric designs were used in a Polish hotel interior on a claim of "public domain" — [Terri Janke](https://www.terrijanke.com.au/post/spotlight-on-first-nations-fashion-and-intellectual-property)
- Kente was originally restricted royal regalia. Context and status are part of its meaning — [Miami University Art Museum](https://sites.miamioh.edu/art-museum/2019/02/kente-cloth-a-look-inside-40-at-40/); [AAIHS](https://www.aaihs.org/the-history-and-significance-of-kente-cloth-in-the-black-diaspora/)

### Inferences (design recommendations for ChromaConscious; not sourced claims)
- Each cultural palette record should carry: endonym and translation, region/community and period, source type (standard / museum artifact / pigment chemistry / modern interpretation), color-value provenance (Munsell/Lab measured vs hex approximated), and a context note (e.g. royal, liturgical, seasonal, mourning).
- Name palettes after **materials and documented systems** ("JIS Z 8102 selection", "Safavid haft-rang glaze set", "iron-tannin bògòlan tones"), not vague ethnic labels ("tribal", "Oriental", "African vibes").
- Ship colors only, never motifs. Exclude sacred/restricted material. Where a tradition is living and community-owned (Aboriginal, kente), add a note pointing to the community's protocols, and prefer palettes contributed or reviewed by members of that community.
- Do not make any culture the default. The agent should pick a palette tradition from the stated audience and purpose, and fall back to the universal-tier rules (perceptual dimensions + CVD safety) when the audience is unknown.

### Gaps
- No HCI study found on how users from source cultures perceive "culturally inspired" UI palettes (respectful vs appropriative).
- No equivalent protocol documents were fetched for West African, Andean or Pacific communities.
